# Creator evidence: what earns the next second

Research checked **5 November 2026**. This document examines creator explanations and platform guidance, not a ranking of the world's largest channels. Publication dates below matter: historical interview claims are not current channel statistics. Transcript timestamps locate the interview evidence; prose articles have section locators instead of invented video timestamps.

**Evidence labels:** **C** = creator's own account in a direct interview; **P** = official platform guidance; **R** = reporter's firsthand account of a creator presentation; **I** = this repository's production inference. None establishes that an editing choice independently caused more views. Public success stories also exclude many unsuccessful videos using similar techniques.

## What the evidence changes about production

The following are **I — proposed production decisions**, not measured promises of reach. They translate the evidence into reviewable work without claiming that every channel uses the same recipe.

1. **Write an attention contract.** Identify the question raised by the opening, the personal consequence that makes it relevant, and the specific answer delivered at the end. Reject an opening that makes a stronger factual claim than the evidence can support.
2. **Make progress visible.** Each story beat should change a goal, constraint, comparison, consequence, or piece of evidence. A camera move counts as presentation; it does not by itself count as new information.
3. **Give the middle causal structure.** Prefer a sequence in which a benefit leads to a cost, the cost affects someone, and their response changes the situation. A list of unrelated alarming facts supplies little reason to await the next beat.
4. **Direct visual attention.** Give every shot a primary subject and one immediately legible action. Background buildings, people, and machinery should establish scale, stakes, or location. Their motion should support the foreground reading order.
5. **Treat speech as performance.** Mark intent—surprise, concern, explanation, contrast, resolution—and place breaths where ideas change. Review whether emphasis matches meaning. Speed changes alone do not solve flat delivery.
6. **Use subtitles as subtitles.** Keep the requested spoken words and alignment. A background box provides contrast; emphasis can highlight a meaningful word while the scene supplies the surprise. Avoid a second competing headline repeating the same idea.
7. **Pay the promise before stopping.** Finish on the answer, meaningful image, or consequence. If a visual loop fits, make the ending recontextualize the opening. Withholding the answer solely to force a second watch is not a satisfying payoff.
8. **Make the next experiment specific.** Record which assumption changed—opening clarity, amount of context, payoff timing, or delivery—and compare like-for-like releases. See [Shorts measurement](shorts-measurement.md) for metric definitions and evaluation limits.

For the data-centre story, a usable original planning question is: **“What changes for the people living next to this new building?”** A visual answer can progress from the proposed facility to residents, infrastructure, and competing local interests. This is a storytelling proposal, not evidence that a particular facility caused any claimed impact; factual assertions still need their own reporting.

| Replay | Optional visual return or detail discovered on rewatch | Is another watch rewarding after the answer is already given? |

## Boundaries of the findings

Jenny explicitly rejects a universal retention target at **18:26** and discusses confounding and high-retention counterexamples at **35:13–36:57** in the [same direct interview](https://creatorscience.com/podcast/jenny-hoyos/). A creator's successful case is a hypothesis source, not an algorithm specification.

These interviews were checked through their published text or transcripts. This document does **not** claim frame-by-frame viewing of every creator's catalogue, access to their private analytics, a causal study, or current worldwide channel rankings. The observations should guide original prototypes and subsequent testing, not guarantees that a template will go viral.
